\section{6. Simulating the sensor values (the physical model)}\label{sec:physics}

BuildSim provides the rooms but nothing that changes. So I simulate the fire, the
air in every room, the sensors and the people here. I never aimed for physical
accuracy. I wanted a model with enough structure that a decision based on its
readings means something. Fires keep growing. Cooking, dust and steam peak and fade.
Smoke leaves through doors and climbs stairwells. Above all, actuator commands
change the next reading, so the loop closes.

\textbf{Sources.} A source is a scripted event with a kind, a room and a start
time. The kind is also the true label that the scoreboard uses. Nothing after the
sensors may read it. Each kind states what it gives off at time $e$ after ignition.
This is a heat release rate, plus the smoke and CO levels the room would settle at
if nothing else happened:

The shape over time is what matters. A real fire keeps getting worse. Cooking, dust
and steam grow, hold and fade. The detector has to pick up that difference over
time, together with the ratio of CO to smoke.

\textbf{Room physics.} I model every space as one lumped node. It has a true
temperature $T$, a smoke obscuration $S$ (how much the smoke blocks light) and a CO
concentration $C$. Each step of $\Delta t$ seconds has two phases. First, each room
moves towards the level its own source pushes it to:
\[
  T \leftarrow T^{\ast} + (T - T^{\ast})\,e^{-\Delta t/\tau_T}, \qquad
  T^{\ast} = T_{\text{out}} + \min\!\Big(140\,\big(q/V\big)^{2/3},\; 900\Big),
\]
Smoke and CO move towards their own equilibrium values in the same first-order way.
The time constants are $\tau_T = 90$\,s, $\tau_S = 45$\,s and $\tau_C = 60$\,s.
$V$ is the room's volume: its floor area from the plan times a 3\,m ceiling. So a
lecture theatre dilutes a fire and a cupboard does not. The temperature rises less
than linearly with heat density, and it is capped at post-flashover temperatures.
Then neighbouring rooms swap what they hold through every doorway and stairwell:
\[
  \Delta X_{a \to b} = \min\!\big(g\,k_X\,\Delta t,\; 0.5\big)\,(X_a - X_b),
  \qquad g = \text{conductance} \times \text{openness},
\]
for $X \in \{T, S, C\}$, with diffusion rates $k_S = 0.35$, $k_C = 0.30$ and
$k_T = 0.12$. The conductance of a doorway comes from BuildSim's walkable graph. It
is 0.6 for a stairwell, and that is how smoke climbs floors. The share a room gives
away in one step is capped across all its doorways together. Without the cap, a
corridor with twenty doors would hand out several times what it holds. The building
would then fill with smoke that nobody produced. That was a real bug. I found it by
running the building at high speed.

\textbf{Closing the loop.} Two actuator states live inside the physics. An active
sprinkler multiplies the room's heat release by 0.25 and its smoke equilibrium by
0.4. A closed fire door sets the openness of every link into that room to 0.15,
because a real fire door leaks. The actuation zone writes both states, and the next
physics step reads them. So a command issued at tick $n$ changes the reading at tick
$n+1$. Section~11 measures what this gains.

Temperature lags most because a heat detector has thermal mass. In a developed
fire, the dashboard shows a reading of 250 while the truth is 648. That is the
clamp at 250\,°C: the detector is saturated, as a real one would be. One live
setting scales the noise. It is 0 for a perfect instrument and 3 for a badly
behaved one, and the evaluation sweeps it. A device can also be given one of four
faults. With \emph{stuck}, it repeats its last value. With \emph{dropout}, it skips
40\,\% of samples. With \emph{drift}, it adds an error that grows without limit.
With \emph{dead}, it reports nothing at all. Faults are set from the Rooms table.
The two hardest presets are built on them.

\textbf{People.} I did not build the daily occupancy schedule from the proposal.
Nothing in fire detection depends on how many people are in a room at nine in the
morning. What matters is where people are when the alarm sounds. So I scatter the
population once over the routable rooms, using a seeded random generator. The
default is 100 students, 10 lecturers, 5 staff, 5 security guards and 10 visitors:
130 people in all. Each role walks at its own multiple of the configured 1.3\,m/s.
The difference is about knowing the building, not fitness. A guard who has drilled
the routes is 15\,\% faster than a student. A visitor who has never been here is
15\,\% slower. Once evacuating, a person follows the waypoints of their route. Each
person carries a fractional effective CO dose, following ISO 13571. This is a
running total of the CO they have breathed in every room they pass through.
Walking out through a smoke-filled corridor adds to it, and the dose stays with
them afterwards. A room stops being escapable at 60\,°C, or when smoke cuts
visibility below 10\,m. The scoreboard counts those rooms as lost.

\section{7. Autonomous services and data engineering}\label{sec:services}

\subsection{7.1 The autonomous service}\label{sec:service}

The intelligence runs once per tick over every monitored room. It has three steps,
and each step sees only the output of the one before. Readings become features.
Features become a probability. The probability drives a state machine. The middle
step has two versions: a hand-written rule and a language model. The operator can
switch between them while a fire is burning. The rule is the simplest thing I found
that meets the requirements. I added the model to find out what a second opinion
costs and what it gains. The end of this section explains how I keep it from
slowing the loop down.

\textbf{Features.} Each monitored room keeps a rolling 120\,s window of readings
for each sensor type. From this window I take the latest smoke, CO and temperature.
I also compute the smoke rate of rise per minute, and the temperature rise above
the building's 20\,°C baseline. Another feature is the ratio of CO to smoke. It is
high for a smoulder and near zero for dust or steam. The last two features are the
share of monitored neighbours that also see smoke, and the coverage. Coverage means
how many sensor types reported at all. Nothing here reads a room's true state. The
test suite fails if that ever changes.

\textbf{The rule.} The default detector is a hand-written fusion rule. A fusion
rule combines several sensor readings into one score. Each feature adds a weighted
term with an upper limit. The terms are summed with a negative bias. The sum then
passes through a logistic function, which squeezes any number into a value between
0 and 1. The result, $P(\text{fire})$, is the detector's estimate of how likely it
is that the room is on fire:
\begin{align*}
  z &= -3.4 + 3.2\,\min(S,1.5) + 1.8\,\tfrac{\max(C-25,\,0)}{125}
       + 1.6\,\min\!\big(\tfrac{C/S}{250},\,2.5\big)\,\gamma
       + 0.55\,\tfrac{\min(\Delta T,\,60)}{10} + 1.4\,\dot S + 0.8\,N, \\
  P(\text{fire}) &= \sigma(z)\cdot\big(0.55 + 0.45\,\tfrac{\min(\text{cov},\,3)}{3}\big),
\end{align*}
where $\gamma = \min(S/0.15, 1)$ keeps the ratio term switched off until there is
enough smoke to divide by. $\dot S$ is the clipped smoke rate and $N$ is the
neighbour agreement. The last factor holds the score back when fewer than three
sensor types are reporting. CO only starts to count above 25\,ppm, which is where
cooking peaks. That is what separates a frying pan from a smoulder. Every term has
an upper limit, so no single sensor type can push the sum to the top on its own.
That is why a dead smoke detector does not blind the system. It is also why a
runaway one does not set off the alarm (Section~11).

The detector is a protocol with two methods, \code{probability} and
\code{explain}. The second one is not optional. The explanation is the calculation
itself. The contributions are the terms above. The dashboard draws them as bars
under the heading ``Why 99\,\%''. Nobody could sign off a detector that cannot say
why. The model has to implement the same two methods, and it does.

\textbf{The alarm ladder.} The agent is one state machine per room. Only the
probability and the clock drive it. The last column is the waiting time:

The waiting times are the defence against false alarms. A single threshold would
flicker on sensor noise and open and close fire doors all day. Holding a level for
a fixed time costs 45\,s on a clear fire. In return, the system stays silent on
cooking, dust and drifting sensors. Standing down takes far longer than raising the
alarm did, and that is on purpose. The thresholds and waiting times are live
settings, so you can explore the trade-off from the panel.

\textbf{What it asks for.} Only two states issue commands. Entering
\textsc{confirmed} asks for the sprinkler on, the fire door closed and the building
evacuated. Entering \textsc{clearing} asks for the reverse. Every command carries a
reason string, such as ``P(fire)=0.91 in A3505'', that goes into the journal. The
agent never writes actuator state. Its requests go to the safety rules. If the
safety rules refuse a request, it stays pending and is offered again every tick.
``Not yet'' must not become ``never''. In the smouldering run, the sprinkler was
refused at 201\,s because there was not enough heat. It was released at 219\,s,
when the room passed 35\,°C. Nobody had to ask twice.

\textbf{Failure and safety.} If the intelligence is wrong, the safety rules still
hold. There is no water without heat. No door is ever locked. No door closes on an
occupied room or an escape route. If the intelligence is unavailable, nothing
moves. Actuators keep their last state, and the building is as safe as it was
without the system. If a person disagrees, supervised mode shows every decision but
carries out none. A manual command from the panel passes through the same safety
rules. It is logged in the journal with the same reasons. If a room has no working
sensor, the detector gives no opinion rather than a low one. So a room without
sensors is never marked safe. None of this changes when the model is the detector.
That is why I kept the safety rules outside the intelligence in the first place.

\textbf{The second detector: a language model.} The proposal planned to compare
the rule with an isolation forest, a standard machine-learning method for spotting
unusual data. I built the comparison with a language model instead, for two
reasons. First, the only labelled data to train a forest on would have been this
simulator's own output. The forest would have learned my physics, not fires.
Second, a model that answers in words can be asked to explain itself. Trained
detectors usually fail that requirement. The adapter in \code{adapters/llm/}
implements the same two-method protocol as the rule. It sends one room's readings
to any OpenAI-compatible chat endpoint. It sends the same features the rule sees,
and nothing else. The system prompt tells the model that it sees only the sensors,
never the room. It also lists what separates a fire from cooking, dust or steam, in
the same terms the rule uses. A flaming fire gets worse on all three sensor types.
A smoulder is dense smoke and high CO with no heat. Cooking is warm and smoky, with
CO under 25\,ppm. Dust and steam are smoke alone. A lone sensor that disagrees with
its neighbours is probably broken. The reply is one JSON object with a probability,
a list of weighted evidence and a one-sentence reason. The adapter parses it
strictly. It refuses anything malformed instead of repairing it.

The real engineering is around the call. The tick has 250\,ms, and the endpoint I
had takes about eight seconds to answer. Figure~\ref{fig:llm} shows the setup.
\code{probability()} never waits. If the model's last answer for the room is
younger than 60 simulated seconds, it returns that answer. Otherwise it returns the
rule's number. At most, it queues a question. A worker task runs beside the tick
loop and sends the queued questions one at a time. The most suspicious room goes
first, ranked by the rule's own score. Each room is asked at most once every ten
simulated seconds. A room whose sensors show nothing is never asked, so a quiet
building costs nothing. Each answer is stamped with the time its readings were
taken, not the time it arrived. That is what makes the 60\,s expiry mean something.
The journal records each hand-over in both directions. It also records every
answer that moves the score by a tenth, together with the model's reason.

\textbf{Pacing.} The first time I ran the model at forty times real time, nothing
happened. Every answer arrived 320 simulated seconds after the question. Every
answer had expired by the time it arrived. So the rule scored every room, while the
panel said ``llm''. This is the failure I had to design against. The fix is not a
longer expiry. An answer a minute old is about a room that has since doubled its
smoke. Instead, the detector measures how long answers take. It then reports the
highest speed at which an answer still arrives with half its life left,
$60 / (2 \times \text{latency})$. Every tick, the engine caps the speed dial there
and writes one journal line about it. With the endpoint I used, that is 3$\times$.
The endpoint answers requests one after another. So asking about two rooms at once
only doubled the latency and halved the cap. That is why only one question is in
flight at a time, and the fire room gets it. Switch back to the rule and the cap
lifts.

\textbf{What the comparison is for.} The rule is the baseline that the model has
to beat on three counts. Does it catch the same fires and ignore the same cooking,
dust and steam? How much later does it act? Is its ``Why'' as easy to check as a
sum of terms? Section~11 reports all three. The model's evidence is drawn as the
same bars. There is one extra bar for the model's answer itself, on the rule's
logit scale (the scale of $z$ before the logistic function). So the two are judged
on the same screen.

\subsection{7.2 The data pipeline}\label{sec:pipeline}

Readings flow through a short in-memory pipeline. It serves four consumers at once:
the detector, the control panel, the scoreboard and the CSV export. It is small.
The proposal included a time-series database and a message broker. I dropped both.
Each one would have been another process to keep in step with a clock running
forty times faster than real time.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Ingestion.} Every device samples the truth at its own interval. It
  appends the reading to its room's window. The window keeps 120\,s of simulated
  time per sensor type, because that is all the detector may look at.
\item \textbf{Features and score.} Once per tick, the features are extracted and
  the detector scores the room. The contributions are kept for the ``Why'' panel,
  and the agent takes a step.
\item \textbf{The flight recorder.} Every 5 simulated seconds, it stores one row
  per monitored room. A row holds the time, the true temperature, smoke and CO, the
  three readings, and P(fire). Each room keeps its last 240 rows, about twenty
  minutes. This is what the charts show.
\item \textbf{The journal.} It logs every state change, and every command with its
  reason. It logs every refusal with the safety rule's reason. It also logs every
  detector answer that moved a room's score by a tenth. That line holds the rule's
  top three terms or the model's reason. The journal keeps the last 200 lines. This
  is where the lessons show up.
\item \textbf{The scoreboard.} It judges each new alarm once, against the source
  labels. The result is a catch with its latency, or a miss after 300\,s without
  confirmation. It can also be a false alarm, together with the cooking, dust or
  steam that caused it. Smoke that came through the door from a real fire next door
  is not a false alarm. A fire on another floor is no excuse.
\item \textbf{Out.} The snapshot is rebuilt once per tick and pushed to every open
  panel over SSE. The whole recorder, with labels, is one GET request away as CSV.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Data quality} is handled at the source, not cleaned afterwards. The
project exists to see what the intelligence does with bad data. A dead device stops
contributing, and the coverage factor lowers the score. A dropout leaves gaps that
the window tolerates. A stuck value flattens the rate term. Retention is short on
purpose. Anything beyond twenty minutes is exported, not stored, and a restart
starts clean.

\textsc{suppressed} is still an alarm. The fire is being held, not put out, and the
requests from the confirmed state still stand. A fire that keeps burning through
the water climbs straight back to \textsc{confirmed}. To stand down, the
probability must stay at or below 0.25 for 90\,s. Then the room needs a further
90\,s of quiet before it stops being flagged as an incident. So a sensor that
recovers for a moment cannot reopen a door onto a fire. The process also has its
own lifecycle, outside this diagram. On startup, the service loads the floors from
BuildSim. It installs the detector named in the configuration. If that detector is
the model, it also starts the model's worker task. Then it scatters the population.
If BuildSim stops answering, the service does not crash. It marks itself
disconnected, keeps simulating, stops publishing, and waits for a reload. Changing
the detector while running stops one worker and starts the other under the tick
lock. So no tick ever sees half a swap. The rooms' alarm states carry over
untouched, because the agent never knew which detector it was listening to.
