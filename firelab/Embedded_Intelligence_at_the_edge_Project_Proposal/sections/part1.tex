\section{1. Summary}\label{sec:summary}

This system watches an office building for fire. It acts on what it finds without
waiting for a person. Emulated smoke, carbon monoxide and temperature detectors
measure the true state of each room, but not perfectly. A detector turns their
readings into a probability of fire for each room. There are two detectors to choose
from. One is a hand-written fusion rule: a fixed formula that combines the sensor
readings into one score. The other is a language model that the system asks over the
network. Each room has its own state machine. It climbs from
\textsc{normal} through \textsc{investigating} and \textsc{pre-alarm} to
\textsc{confirmed}. When a room is confirmed, the system releases the sprinkler and
closes the fire door. It also sends everyone out of the building along routes that
avoid the alarming rooms. The building, its rooms and its 3D view come from BuildSim.
This project adds the fire physics, the sensors, the intelligence and the response
around it.

One principle guides every decision below: \textbf{nothing that comes after the
sensors is allowed to see the truth}. The physics knows the real temperature of every
room. The detector, the agent and the safety rules (interlocks) only see what the
sensors reported. The whole project studies the gap between those two views. The
dashboard shows the two side by side. So a false alarm or a missed fire shows up as a
mismatch on screen, not hidden in a log.

\textbf{Changes since the proposal.} Three things changed. Each one changed because
building the system taught me something the proposal did not know.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{One process instead of a dozen.} The proposal sketched nine services
  joined by MQTT, with ThingsBoard for storage and dashboards. Its own risk list said
  the hardest part would be keeping a dozen processes in step in simulated time.
  That risk turned out to be the whole problem. At forty times real time, a reading
  sent over a broker is already out of date when the subscriber sees it. The
  physics, the sensors and the agent must agree on what ``now'' is. Otherwise a
  waiting time means nothing. So I built the system as one tick loop with four zones
  inside it. The proposal wanted the parts kept apart by process boundaries. I keep
  them apart with import rules instead, and a test checks those rules
  (Section~\ref{sec:container}). Section~\ref{sec:risks} discusses the trade-off.
\item \textbf{The second detector is a language model, not an isolation forest.}
  The proposal planned to compare a hand-written fusion rule with a trained
  anomaly detector. The rule alone caught every real fire. It raised no false alarm
  at any noise level I tested (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). The only data to
  train a forest on would have been the simulator's own output. So I made the
  promised comparison with a different second opinion: a language model behind an
  OpenAI-compatible endpoint. The model gets the same readings the rule sees and
  nothing else. I ask it for a probability, its evidence and a reason. It follows
  the same two-method protocol, so the agent, the safety rules and the dashboard did
  not change. Section~\ref{sec:service} says how I keep it off the tick.
  Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} reports the comparison. In short, the model catches
  what the rule catches, but later and much more slowly. Its answers are also not
  repeatable. The rule stays the default.
\item \textbf{Holding smoke back versus getting people out became a safety rule, not a policy.}
  The proposal expected to tune a trade-off. In practice the safe rule is simple,
  and it should not be tunable. A fire door may not close on a room that is occupied
  or that lies on someone's escape route. Doors never lock. The agent asks and the
  safety rule decides. The request stands until it is safe to carry out.
\end{itemize}

\section{2. Use case and building context (BuildSim)}\label{sec:usecase}

\textbf{The building.} The system runs on the A-building at Luleå University of
Technology, as BuildSim models it. It has three storeys, 956 named spaces, 1005
doorways and 32 stairwells. It has seven real exits, all on the ground floor.
BuildSim owns the floor plans and the walkable graph. This graph joins rooms through
their doors, and joins the storeys through the stairwells. BuildSim also owns an
equipment tree, where devices are registered, and a 3D viewer fed over WebSocket. I
did not modify BuildSim. I only added sensors to it and drew on it.

\textbf{What BuildSim does not provide} is anything that changes over time. There
is no fire, no smoke, no people and no sensor physics. This project adds all of that.
firelab loads the floor plans once. It turns them into a graph of rooms linked
through their doors, where an open or closed door changes the link. From then on, it
writes back to BuildSim about once per simulated second. It writes floor heat maps of
the true temperature, smoke and CO. It writes particle effects for fire, smoke and
running sprinklers. It also writes the agent's alarm markers, the fire-door state,
every occupant's position, and each device's latest reading into the equipment tree.
Two things go into the viewer session instead of the building, because BuildSim
keeps them per browser tab. These are the coloured outlines of alarming rooms and
the escape route.

\textbf{Actors.} The \textbf{operator} watches the control panel. The operator
chooses what to set on fire, injects sensor faults and picks which detector scores
the rooms. The operator can also switch the system into supervised mode. In that
mode the agent still decides, but a person has to press the button. The
\textbf{occupants} are simulated people. They affect nothing until the alarm sends
them walking. If someone is in a room, that room's door cannot be closed. Neither
actor is on the control path. The loop from reading to sprinkler runs on its own.

\textbf{The scenario the demo tells.} A fast flaming fire starts in office A3505
on the top floor. Fifty-one seconds later, the smoke reading has risen enough for the
room to start \textsc{investigating}. At 81\,s it is in \textsc{pre-alarm}. At
106\,s the room \textsc{confirms}. By then the probability of fire has stayed above
0.75 for the full confirmation waiting time. Three things happen in the same tick.
The sprinkler is released, because the temperature reading backs up the smoke. The
fire door closes, because the room is empty and on nobody's route. All 130 people in
the building start walking to a ground-floor exit, on routes that avoid the alarming
rooms. The last person is out 111\,s after the alarm. By contrast, the
kitchen-cooking preset in the same room makes smoke and warmth for fifteen minutes.
It never gets past \textsc{investigating}, because its CO never rises.

\textbf{Why it matters.} A smoke detector cannot tell a fire from burnt toast, a
dusty corridor or its own drifting element. Real buildings are evacuated for all
three. As a result, people learn to ignore alarms. Telling these apart takes several
sensor types, combined over time and across neighbouring rooms. That is a real job
for an autonomous service. The response has a real conflict inside it too. Closing a
fire door holds the smoke back, but it also takes away an escape route. Three things
drive the requirements that follow: how fast the system reacts, how well it copes
with broken sensors, and how safe its actions are.

\textbf{Where it sits.} Fire detection is one of several systems that could run
on the same building. The others in the course are ventilation, security and energy
management. Each one adds sensors to BuildSim through the same API. The same
facilities staff would watch all of them. Figure~\ref{fig:landscape} shows the
landscape. This document designs the highlighted system. One part of it reaches
beyond the laptop. When the language model is the chosen detector, the service asks
a chat endpoint over HTTPS about one room at a time. This is optional. Without it,
or when it is slow, the rule scores every room.

\section{3. Requirements}\label{sec:requirements}

Every requirement is a testable statement with a fixed ID. Functional requirements
(FR) say what the system does. Safety requirements (SR) say what it must never do,
whatever the agent decides. Non-functional requirements (NFR) say how well it must
work. The last column names the test or experiment that checks each one. The test
plan in Section~10 and the results in Section~11 close the loop.

There is a tension in this table. FR-01 and FR-02 want the alarm early.
FR-03 wants it late enough to be sure. SR-01 to SR-03 may refuse the response the
alarm asks for. The waiting times in the state machine settle the first tension. The
safety rules settle the second. The evaluation reports where each of these
compromises costs something. NFR-06 and NFR-07 also pull against each other. A
detector that answers eight seconds late is useless at forty times real time. So
either the clock slows down or the rule takes over. The system does the first and
falls back to the second. The safety requirements apply whichever detector is
installed. That is why I keep the safety rules outside the intelligence.
