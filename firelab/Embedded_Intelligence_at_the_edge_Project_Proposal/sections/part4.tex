\section{9. Deployment and component placement}

Everything runs on one laptop, so deployment is about how the system is composed
from processes rather than where in the world they are. There are two processes
and two browser tabs, and they talk only through the interfaces of Section~5.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{images/deploy.png}
\caption{Deployment view. Two processes, two tabs, loopback only. The dotted box is the one thing that would ever leave the edge.}
\label{fig:deploy}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Why this placement.} The control loop, from physics through sensors to
the interlocks and back, lives in the firelab process and touches nothing outside
the machine except BuildSim over loopback. In a real building that process is the
edge node: it must keep working when the internet does not, and it must answer
inside the tick budget, which Section~11 measures. The only work that would ever
belong elsewhere is training a detector on recorded runs, which is slow, offline
and off the critical path; that is why it is drawn dotted and why the CSV export
exists. BuildSim stands in for the building's own controllers and is left
alone.

\textbf{What is lost by merging the zones into one process}, since the course
asks: fault isolation and independent restart. If the physics throws, the agent
dies with it. That is acceptable for a simulator on a laptop and would not be for a
building. The mitigation built in is the layering, which keeps the zones as
separable as separate processes would, and the seam is known: the sensing zone
already produces per-device readings keyed by device ID, and the actuation zone
already consumes commands with a reason, which are the two messages a broker
would carry.

\textbf{Operational notes.} The service is started with \code{make run}, which
creates a virtual environment, builds the panel and serves it. Configuration is
live: speed, thresholds, dwells, sensor noise and population change on the next
tick through \code{PUT /api/config}, and a reload re-reads the floors from BuildSim
without restarting. If BuildSim is down at startup the service says so in its
journal and idles until a reload; if it goes away mid-run the service marks itself
disconnected, stops writing, and keeps simulating.

\textbf{The blind spot.} The preset with a dead smoke detector is the one result
that did not go to plan. Heat and CO alone pushed the fire room to a probability of
0.748, held there by the coverage factor, which caps a two-modality room at 85\,\%
of its logistic score. The confirm threshold is 0.75. The room sat in
\textsc{pre-alarm} for the whole run, three thousandths short. The building was
still evacuated at 156\,s, because smoke crossed into A3500A next door, whose
three working detectors confirmed and whose neighbour-agreement term the fire room
fed. Two lessons: the neighbour cross-check is not decoration, it is what caught
this fire; and the coverage penalty is tuned a hair too hard. Lowering it from 0.55
to 0.60 at the floor, or letting a neighbour's confirmation lift the room, would
have confirmed the room itself. This is the first change I would make.

around the fire, not of the fire room alone. Without any response, nobody is told
to leave and the RSET is never recorded, which is the simplest argument for the
evacuate command being automatic.

Detection latency did not move by a second across the noise sweep, and no

\textbf{Scaling and the breaking point (E5).} The tick loop wakes every 250\,ms
of real time and must finish before it wakes again. At 1$\times$ speed one tick is one
physics step over the whole building plus one intelligence pass over the monitored
rooms; at 40$\times$ it is forty physics steps and one pass.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{images/chart_scaling.png}
\caption{Wall time of one engine tick against the number of monitored rooms (E5), on the laptop the demo runs on. The building has 956 rooms and 1005 doorways.}
\label{fig:scaling}
\end{figure}

At real time the whole building fits with room to spare: 23\,ms per tick with all
956 rooms monitored and 2868 devices sampling. At 40$\times$ the budget is crossed at
about 450 monitored rooms. The shape says where the cost is. With only 10 rooms
monitored a 40$\times$ tick already takes 180\,ms, so the forty physics steps over
the full graph are the fixed cost, about 4.5\,ms a step; the intelligence adds about
0.18\,ms per monitored room on top. The physics does not scale with the number of
sensors, so the fix for a bigger building or a faster clock is a sparser diffusion
step, not a faster detector. When the budget is exceeded nothing breaks: the loop
runs slower than the speed dial claims, and the tick caps itself at sixty
physics steps so a stall cannot snowball.

draws both on one chart. A saturated heat detector, a stuck smoke detector or a
drifting one is visible as a gap between two lines, which is the thing an operator
of a real building never gets to see and the thing this project is about.
Beside it the ``Why'' bars decompose the probability into its contributions, so a
99\,\% is not a claim but a sum. The journal below records every state change,
every command with its reason, and every refusal with the interlock's reason, and
the scorecard grades the run as it happens: catches, misses, false alarms, the alarm
delay and the RSET. Everything on the panel arrives over the event stream, one
snapshot per tick, and the whole record leaves as a labelled CSV.

\section{13. Risks and critical reflection}

The system does what the requirements ask, and the evaluation found four things
that deserve more scepticism than a tick mark.

\textbf{The dead-detector blind spot is real.} A room with two of three
modalities working stalled three thousandths below the confirm threshold, and was
rescued by its neighbour. That is a good outcome in this building, where offices
share doorways with corridors, but it depends on the building's layout rather than on the detector. The coverage
penalty should be softened, or a confirmed neighbour should be allowed to confirm
the room. Both are one-line changes; choosing between them needs more runs than
one.

\textbf{The margins are thinner than the zeros suggest.} No nuisance alarmed, but
cooking reached 0.749 against a threshold of 0.75. The dwells are what actually
kept it quiet. A different kitchen, a bigger pan, or a slightly different noise
draw would push the probability over; whether it would then hold for 45 seconds is
the real question, and it was not measured across enough seeds to say. The false-alarm rate reported here is zero in the runs performed, not
zero.

\textbf{Containment is modelled too kindly to the neighbours and too harshly to
the fire room.} Closing the doors halved the smoke next door, which is right, and
raised the fire room from 309 to 648\,°C, which is a consequence of a model in which
water only reduces heat release and a sealed room cannot run out of oxygen. In a
zone model the sprinkler would cool the gas layer and the fire would starve. The
relative comparisons in Section~11 stand; the absolute temperatures should not be
quoted. The sprinkler interlock's 35\,°C bar is also low, and the smoulder cleared
it in 18\,s; a rate-of-rise condition would be more defensible.

\textbf{One process is the right call for a simulator and the wrong one for a
building.} The proposal wanted nine containers and the report has one. The
justification, one simulated clock, is genuine, and the layering test keeps the
zones separable. But nothing here demonstrates fault isolation, and a fire safety
system that dies with its physics engine would not be certified. The migration
path is known and narrow: the sensing zone already emits per-device readings and
the actuation zone already consumes reasoned commands, which are the two topics a
broker would carry, and simulated time would travel as a field in every message.
The evacuation model is optimistic in the same direction: 130 people out in 111\,s
is a walking-speed number with no queueing at doors and no one who hesitates.
